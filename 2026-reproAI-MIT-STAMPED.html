STAMPED, 2026 — generalized, gradeable, domain-agnostic

We did not invent these for AI. That is the point — they were already the right properties.

Seven properties a research object should have —
whoever, or whatever, picks it up next.

Let the banner sit for a beat before advancing the fragment. Banner art is the STAMPED project's own (stamped-principles.org).

STAMPED in one table

Property In one line What it buys an agent
Self-contained Everything under one boundary A bounded context window. Nothing it needs is “on Bob’s laptop”.
Tracked Version control & provenance A diff to review, and a record of who/what produced each change — including the agent.
Actionable Executable & documented workflows The load-bearing one. A command it can run, not a paragraph it must interpret.
Modular Independent & composable modules A unit small enough to reason about, swap, and verify in isolation.
Portable Runs anywhere with explicit specs The agent’s sandbox is not your laptop. If it only runs on your laptop, it does not run.
Ephemeral Transient compute environment Throw-away containers — exactly how agents already work. No accreted state to corrupt.
Distributable Shareable, persistent, retrievable It can get the inputs itself instead of asking you for a Dropbox link.

A. Macdonald, C. Baker, I. To, Y. O. Halchenko. STAMPED: principles for reproducible research objects. Preprint, 2026. doi: 10.31222/osf.io/f3h82_v1  ·  sources: stamped-principles/stamped-paper  ·  paper.stamped-principles.org

Do not read the table. Say: seven properties, each a spectrum not a pass/fail gate, each with MUST/SHOULD/MAY requirements under it. Then jump straight to the third column as the reason this audience should care.

Actionability is the one that agents live or die on

An actionable object answers, without a human in the loop:

  • What is this? → a declared standard (BIDS, NWB), not a README paragraph
  • How do I run it? → a recorded command, not “see Methods”
  • Did I get it right? → a validator exit code, not a reviewer’s opinion
And standards are what make Actionability cheap. You do not negotiate the layout per project — you inherit it.
“You’ve seen one BIDS dataset, you’ve seen them all” was a convenience for humans. For a model that would otherwise have to infer the layout, it is the difference between acting and hallucinating.

“All standards are ‘bad’, but some are used.” — D. Clunie, MICCAI 2017. A used standard beats a correct one — and now it has a second constituency.

This is the slide Dave's title was pointing at. Say it plainly: structure is not bureaucracy, it is the interface that lets a non-human collaborator participate without going wild. Then: but "actionable" did not stay still — next slide.

… and AI changed what counts as actionable

“Actionable” required… … so what could act
Before An exact command, API call, or documented CLI. Prose was not enough. Tools, and humans who already knew the tool. Everything else stayed a paragraph in the Methods.
Now A procedure described in human language — with scripts dropped in exactly where precision matters. Any agent, on first contact. The interface moved from syntax to language, so vastly more of what we already wrote down became executable.
Next Routines that fire without being asked. The harness itself — CI, PR monitors, scheduled agents.
1. Skills — logic written in language
  • Human language carries the judgement; scripts carry the precision
  • One skill, one job — modularity still applies
  • The procedure you did by hand three times, written down once
 2. Routines — the harness runs them for you
  • Claude Code on the web watching a repo: triage issues, review PRs
  • Scheduled agents; CI that does more than run tests
  • No human has to remember to invoke it
AI did not only make more things actionable. It made automation on top of actionability cheap — which is the real multiplier.

→ Michelle already showed you this middle row working. My claim is about why it works now and did not five years ago: the unit of automation stopped being a CLI and became a described procedure.

The escalation is the point: actionable -> automatable. Before, you automated only what already had a clean CLI. Now the unit of automation can be a described procedure, so the set of things worth automating exploded. That is the opportunity — and the next slide is the bill that comes with it.

So grade them too: a skill is a research object

A skill that only runs in your repo, on your paths, undocumented and unversioned, is a silo with a friendlier interface.

Property … applied to a skill or an automation
Self-contained Carries its own references and scripts. Not “see our internal wiki”.
Tracked In git, with a metadata.version and a history — so a change to the procedure is reviewable.
Actionable Runnable commands and scripts, not only prose. Recursive, and the point.
Modular One skill, one job. Composable with the others instead of a mega-prompt.
Portable No hardcoded paths, no “works on our cluster”. Declares its dependencies and versions.
Ephemeral Runs in a fresh sandbox. No accreted state it silently depends on.
Distributable Published where someone else can find and install it — not pasted into chat.
Good automation and good skills pass the STAMPED checklist. That is not a purity test — it is precisely what makes them reusable beyond the person who wrote them.

Walk it yourself: checklist.stamped-principles.org (MUST / SHOULD / MAY, item by item) · examples · principles · paper source (main.tex — small enough to paste into a context window, which is how we actually grade a study today).

The missing piece: con/duct

Wrap any command. It records, next to the outputs: wall time, CPU, peak RSS, per-process-tree samples, exit code, environment, full stdout/stderr — as plain, self-contained files.

… and that log is the provenance you need later

“Why did this get OOM-killed on the cluster?”
  • Peak RSS over time, per process. Not your memory of it.
“How much do I request for 2,000 subjects?”
  • Measured, from the run you already did.
“What did it print before it died?”
  • Captured, versioned, next to the outputs.

con-duct plot — MRIQC on one subject/session
Why an AI audience should care: when you hand a failing pipeline to an agent, it needs numbers and text it can read, not a human recollection. duct turns “it crashed, I think it ran out of memory” into a file. Self-contained (S), tracked (T), immediately actionable (A).

Agent = Model + Harness

formulation borrowed from Oscar Esteban, OHBM 2026

Harness piece What it is STAMPED property
AGENTS.md / CLAUDE.md The project’s operational contract: conventions, commands, what not to do Self-contained
Skills A named, reusable procedure with its own references and scripts Modular, Actionable
Hooks / CI Guard rails that run whether or not anyone remembered to Tracked
MCP servers The port to external systems — archives, trackers, databases Distributable
Container / sandbox
(con/yolo)		 Where it may run and how much it can break — and the one piece that enforces self-containment: what you did not declare is not in there Self-contained, Portable, Ephemeral
Pre-flight check Credentials, access and inputs verified before the run starts — fail in the first second, not in hour three Actionable, Ephemeral
The harness is itself a research object. Which means it can be self-contained, tracked, modular, portable — or it can be a pile of prompts in your notes app.
Two of these rows are worth a sentence each. The sandbox is not only blast-radius control. It is the one piece that ENFORCES self-containment rather than asking for it: inside a fresh container, anything you forgot to declare is not there, so the property gets checked on every run instead of being a virtue you remember. That is the same move as a validator -- turn a discipline into something the machine fails on. Pre-flight is John Lee's point, and it is the cheap one: before a study or a long agent session starts, assert that every credential, token and access path it will need is actually present. Fail in the first second rather than in hour three with half a dataset written. Honest caveat if anyone asks: not everything can be pre-flighted. Approvals that are granted interactively on first use -- a hook authorising against an external service, say -- cannot be checked ahead of time, so pre-flight covers what is checkable and the rest still bites you.

Two regimes — and be honest about which one you are in

Either the system checks itself against goals and spec — or the work items stay small enough that a human still can.

A. Scaffolded self-verification
the agentic system keeps itself in sync
  • An executable spec the loop is checked against (spec-kit, OpenSpec, LAD)
  • Tests and validators as the oracle — the loop must be able to fail
  • Provenance so drift is detectable at all (datalad run, duct, CI history)
  • Automated gates before human eyes; humans review intent and architecture
Only then are large autonomous batches defensible.
B. Small, human-reviewable, hand-tried
where almost every research project actually is
  • A diff a human can actually read is a design constraint, not a courtesy
  • One concern per change; small commits; mark them (@pytest.mark.ai_generated)
  • Run it by hand before you believe it. Agent-rate output, human-rate trust
  • Cheap to back out — the reversibility is the safety mechanism
Not a lesser mode — the correct default until A is actually built.
There is no third regime where you ship large, unverified agent output safely. And the way out of B is not a better model — it is Actionable specs, Tracked provenance and Modular units: the same STAMPED properties, again.

“Well, less is more” — Robert Browning, Andrea del Sarto, 1855; later the motto of Mies van der Rohe, who credited his mentor Peter Behrens rather than claiming it.

This is the slide to slow down on. The claim is not "AI bad" — it is that the size of a safe work item is set by the strength of your verification loop, and nothing else. Most research projects have a weak loop, so their safe work item is small. That is fine; it is only dangerous when unacknowledged. Next slide shows this is not just our opinion.

Take home — what to do Monday

  1. Make one object actionable. Pick one study. Put it in a declared standard (BIDS, NWB) and run the validator. The agent gets a grammar; you get a type-checker.
  2. Record one command. Wrap your next analysis step in datalad run — or at minimum in duct. A provenance record you did not have to remember to write.
  3. Grade one thing against STAMPED. checklist.stamped-principles.org, fifteen minutes, and you will know which property is your weakest.
  4. Write down your harness. An AGENTS.md in the repo beats a prompt in your notes app. Declare your AI policy there while you are at it.
  5. Size the work to the loop you actually have. Until the project self-checks, keep AI-assisted items small, reviewable, and tried by your own hand — and say so out loud, so nobody mistakes regime B for regime A.
  6. Push one skill upstream. The next person who needs your procedure should clone it, not re-derive it. scientific-agent-skills, con/skills, or your own repo — but somewhere public.
Standards and provenance were always the right answer. AI did not change the answer — it changed the deadline.

STAMPED and FAIR do different jobs

FAIR STAMPED
About sharing: can someone else find this and get it? About the object being operational on its own: can someone — or something — re-run it?
A FAIR dataset can still be a .zip of .mat files with a DOI. A STAMPED object carries its own layout, environment, and execution record.
Graded by a repository. Graded by a checklist you can walk, item by item.
Not competitors — different lenses on the same stack. FAIR tells you whether the world can reach it. STAMPED tells you whether anything useful happens once it does.
